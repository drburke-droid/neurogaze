About this simulator

The Interactive Gaze Simulator shows how the six extraocular muscles and three cranial nerves move the eyes, and what happens when one of them fails. Move the fixation target and watch both eyes follow. Then apply a nerve palsy, a restrictive disorder or a supranuclear lesion and compare the eyes in the nine diagnostic positions of gaze.

It is built for optometry, ophthalmology and neurology students and for clinicians explaining double vision to patients.

How to use it

  • Look around: move the pointer over the face, drag with a finger, or use the 3×3 pad. Keys 1–9 match a numeric keypad; arrow keys nudge the gaze.
  • Lock the gaze: click the face to freeze the eyes where you clicked, so you can move to the controls without changing the gaze. Click again to unlock.
  • Pick a case from the list and choose the affected side. The card explains the signs and the red flags.
  • Fixing eye: switch fixation to the affected eye to see the larger secondary deviation predicted by Hering's law.
  • Near target: shows convergence, which is spared in internuclear ophthalmoplegia.
  • Compare with normal: press and hold anywhere on the face (or hold the N key) to flip to a healthy patient in the same gaze.
  • Share: the address bar always holds the current case and gaze, so "Copy link" opens exactly what you see. "Save image" downloads a labelled picture.

Conditions in the simulator

Sixth nerve (abducens) palsy

The eye cannot turn outward. Esotropia is largest when looking toward the affected side and small looking away. Check for papilloedema: raised intracranial pressure can cause it.

Fourth nerve (trochlear) palsy

The affected eye sits higher, most of all when looking down and toward the nose. Patients tilt the head to the opposite shoulder. The Parks–Bielschowsky three-step test confirms it.

Third nerve (oculomotor) palsy

The eye rests down and out, with limited adduction, elevation and depression, usually with ptosis. A dilated pupil or pain suggests an aneurysm and needs urgent imaging.

Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)

An MLF lesion stops the eye on the lesion side from adducting on side gaze, with nystagmus of the abducting eye. Convergence is usually spared. Often multiple sclerosis in the young, stroke in older patients.

Thyroid eye disease

Tight, enlarged muscles restrict movement away from them. The inferior and medial recti are most often involved, limiting elevation and abduction.

Orbital floor (blowout) fracture

Entrapment of the inferior rectus limits elevation. A child with entrapment, nausea and a slow heart rate needs urgent surgical review.

Brown syndrome

A tight superior oblique tendon stops the eye elevating when it is turned in. Elevation looking outward is near normal.

Duane syndrome

A congenital miswiring: the eye cannot abduct, and on adduction it retracts into the orbit. Double vision is rare.

Myasthenia gravis

Weakness that worsens with use and recovers with rest, mimicking any palsy. Hold a gaze in the simulator and watch it fade.

Miller Fisher syndrome

A Guillain–Barré variant with ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia, usually after an infection.

Skew deviation (Wallenberg syndrome)

A supranuclear vertical misalignment: the eye on the side of a lateral medullary stroke sits lower, by about the same amount in every gaze. Acute vertical diplopia with vertigo is a stroke until proven otherwise.

What the model does and does not show

This is a qualitative teaching model, not a biomechanical simulation. It limits eye movements to physiological ranges, separates weak muscles (paresis) from tight ones (restriction), applies Hering's law through a selectable fixing eye, and treats MLF and skew lesions as supranuclear. It does not model eyelids, pupils, torsion, head tilt or saccade velocities, and the numbers it displays are illustrative.
